On the wedding day I got to the church much earlier than I needed to. It wasn't a mistake in the schedule; it was on purpose. I wanted time to get the drone up without rushing, circle the church from the air and catch the shots there's no time for later, once the guests arrive.

That morning, arriving early turned out to be useful for a reason I hadn't planned.

## The key that wouldn't work

I parked, took out my gear and locked the car. A few minutes later, when I went back for another bag, the key no longer responded. The battery in the key had died — that very morning, after who knows how many months without a problem.

On an ordinary day, that's a minor thing. On a wedding day, when after the church I have to get to the reception, and after that who knows where else, it's a problem that can't wait. I didn't want to risk being stuck without a car in the middle of the day, so I went to buy a new battery and replaced it straight away.

If I had arrived “on time”, this would have eaten into the filming. As it was, it only ate into the buffer I had deliberately left myself.

## The church from the air, while it's still quiet

When I got back, there was still no one around the church. That's exactly the moment for the drone. No guests, no cars pulling up, no rush. I could circle the church at my own pace, try out a few flight paths, wait for the sun to come out from behind a cloud and repeat a shot I didn't like.

Shots like these later open the film in the edit: the church slowly revealing itself, the roof, the bell tower, the churchyard, the surroundings. They place the viewer in the setting before the story begins. And once the guests arrive, the drone comes down and doesn't fly again — that's the time for the camera on the ground.

## The empty church inside

After the drone, I went inside with the camera. An empty church is especially beautiful to film: silence, pews in rows, candles, frescoes. In those few minutes I slowly film details without people in the shot, which I later use as a pause between the key moments of the ceremony.

It's also when I check the light. And the light in a church is, honestly, some of the most demanding of the whole wedding.

## Light that fights itself

Strong sunlight comes in through the windows, while the walls covered in frescoes are dark. The difference between the brightest and darkest parts of the frame is enormous — far bigger than the eye notices while you're standing inside.

The camera can't hold on to everything. By setting the shutter speed and the aperture, I choose what will be correctly exposed in the footage. If I expose for the windows, they look perfect, but the people and the frescoes sink into darkness. If I open the aperture enough to see faces clearly, the windows burn out to white.

For me, there's no dilemma: people's faces always matter more than windows. In ten years, no one will remember what the window behind the altar looked like. Everyone will want to see the bride's face as she says “I do”.

That's why I also use that morning to test settings in the spots where the couple will stand, while the church is still empty. Once the ceremony begins, there's no time for a rehearsal.

## Why the videographer arrives early

When people ask me why I get there so far ahead of everyone, I now have a specific story to tell. Arriving early is a buffer for the unexpected — a key that won't work, a closed access road, anything that can happen on a wedding day. And when nothing happens, I turn that time into shots no one would have had time to film later.
