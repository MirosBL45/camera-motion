Some shoots you remember for the footage. This one I also remember because the drone never came back from it. I'm writing about it honestly, because that mistake taught me a lesson I've applied every time I've filmed from a boat since — and because, in the end, the story turned out well for the client.

## The brief: a speedboat at full speed

The client was a boat rental company on the Adriatic, near Zadar. They needed footage of a yacht — a speedboat, to be precise — at speed on the open sea: a boat cutting through the waves, foam behind the stern, the sea and the coastline in the background. I was filming with a DJI Mavic Air.

On paper it sounds simple. In practice, when a drone takes off from and lands on a moving boat, everything gets a little harder.

## Balance on deck, eyes on the screen

The first thing I learned that day was how hard it is to stand on a rocking speedboat and fly a drone at the same time. Your eyes are on the remote's screen the whole time — you have to follow both the drone and the shot, and keep the boat in frame while both of you are racing along. Meanwhile you can't see where your feet are, and the deck is moving under you.

I built the shots on the go: the drone ahead of the boat, then alongside it, then high above as the boat shrinks on the blue surface. After a few passes you get used to the rhythm of the waves, but at no point does it become relaxed.

## Sun, water and white

The second challenge was the light. The sun reflects off the water and off the white parts of the yacht, creating strong glare in the footage. The eye handles it easily, but the camera records it as blown-out white patches.

You don't see that while filming, but you do in the edit. Grading footage like that takes more time: recovering detail where possible and matching shots taken from different angles to the sun.

## A return that set off the other way

When the shoot was over, I activated RTH — the function that automatically flies the drone back to its starting point. The problem was what that starting point was. The home point had stayed saved at the spot where the drone took off. And in the meantime, the speedboat had moved, quite a lot.

Quite logically, the drone headed for the old point — somewhere out on the open sea, where there was no longer anything.

I took control and flew it back towards the boat manually. Then again, and again. On one of those attempts, the drone hit the yacht and fell into the sea.

## What was saved

The drone was pulled out of the water and dried thoroughly, but by then the salt had damaged the electronics and it never worked again. What mattered most, though, survived: the memory card was intact and all the footage on it was saved.

The client got the material they had asked for and was happy in the end. For me it was an expensive day, but the job was done.

## The lesson

When filming from a moving boat, you don't leave the home point where you took off. Right before the return, update it to the current position of the remote — in other words, to wherever the boat is at that moment.

And second, perhaps more important: RTH is not an automatic return to the boat. It flies the drone back to the point it remembers, not to where you are. On land, that's usually the same place. At sea, it almost never is.
