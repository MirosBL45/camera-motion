Most weddings are planned for spring and summer, in the hope of a sunny day. But more and more couples are choosing winter, and summer is no guarantee either — a summer downpour has a way of arriving just as you walk out of the church. The good news is that bad weather doesn't mean a bad film. It just means a different plan.

In [What drone wedding videography looks like](blog:snimanje-svadbe-dronom) we touched on the weather only briefly. Here we go into detail: what can realistically be filmed when the weather won't cooperate.

## When the drone doesn't fly

Let's start with what isn't possible. A drone doesn't fly in rain, and it doesn't fly in strong wind. Water damages the electronics, and wind makes a drone unpredictable — and that's reason enough for it to stay on the ground, however beautiful the shot would be.

It isn't a decision we make at the last minute. We follow the forecast for days before the wedding, and on the day itself we check conditions before every flight. Sometimes a half-hour window opens between two showers, and then we fly. Sometimes it doesn't, and then the drone stays down.

## Plan B: the camera on the ground and the interior

When the drone can't fly, the weight of the film shifts to the camera on the ground — which carries the most important part of the day anyway. Emotions, faces, vows, speeches and dancing don't depend on the weather. They happen indoors, and there the camera works just as it would in sunshine.

What changes is what we show instead of the view from above. We give more attention to the interior: the church, the venue, the decorations, the tables, the lights. Rain on the window, umbrellas at the church door and droplets on the car can become part of the story instead of spoiling it. A film that honestly shows what the day was like is often more beautiful than one that tries to hide the rain.

## In winter, the day is shorter

A winter wedding has one rule that changes the whole schedule: there's far less light. The sun sets early, and after that, outdoor shots and drone flights are no longer possible.

So we plan everything filmed outdoors for earlier in the day. Aerial shots of the church and the surroundings, the walk and the shots of the two of you need to fit into the part of the day while it's still light. If the ceremony is late in the afternoon, that sometimes means the couple shots happen before the ceremony, or that time is found for them right after you leave the church.

We talk about this when we put the schedule together. The sooner we know what your day looks like, the easier it is to find those precious minutes of light.

## Cold and batteries

Cold has another consequence people rarely think about: batteries run down faster. That's true for the drone as well as for cameras and microphones. Below freezing, a drone battery lasts noticeably less than in summer.

For you that changes nothing, but for us it means preparing differently — with more spare batteries, kept warm, and with shorter flights. That's why in winter it matters even more that the aerial shots are agreed in advance, so that every flight counts.

## Snow, fog and overcast skies

Winter isn't only a problem — it can also be a gift. Snow turns even the most ordinary landscape into something special, and from the air a white surface gives you a shot you'll never get in summer. Fog lying over a river or a field creates an atmosphere no filter can recreate. Even an overcast sky has an advantage: soft, even light without harsh shadows, in which faces look lovely and natural.

Some of the most beautiful shots couples have were taken on a day they had considered bad in advance.

The most important thing is to decide together before the wedding what we'll film if the weather doesn't allow a flight: which shots move indoors, what the camera films instead of the drone, and what matters most to you to capture no matter what.
